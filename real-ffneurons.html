The neurons that recognize an idea.

Induction split open attention — the part where words look at each other. But that's only half of every layer. The other half is the feed-forward step, where each word is processed on its own — and it holds two-thirds of the whole model's numbers. What is all that machinery for? Here we go looking, inside real GPT-2, for a single unit that does something you can name: recognize that a word is a country.

What we're poking at

The 3,072 little dials inside every word

After the words have looked at each other, each word's vector goes through one more step on its own: it's widened from 768 numbers to 3,072, bent (each of the 3,072 is passed through a curve), then shrunk back to 768. Those 3,072 numbers in the middle are the closest thing a model has to neurons. Each one is a dial: mostly near zero, but for certain inputs it swings up — it “fires.” Our whole question is simple: does any single dial fire for a specific, nameable idea? To find out, we watch the dials in the model's very first layer while we feed it words.

Why it has to be messy — the counting problem

A whole layer has 3,072 dials; the whole model, 36,864. But a language model has to tell apart far more than 36,864 things — every object, place, tense, tone, name, and relationship in the language. There aren't enough dials to give each idea its own. So the model is forced to pack several ideas into one dial, and spread one idea across several. A few ideas (like “country”) happen to get a nearly-clean dial; most are smeared across many. This packing is called superposition, and it's the single biggest reason reading a model's mind is hard — untangling it is an open research frontier.

Why this matters
  • This is where the model keeps what it knows. Attention moves information between words (that's induction); these feed-forward dials are where a word gets recognized and enriched — “this is a country,” “this is a color.” It's no accident two-thirds of the parameters live here.
  • Real concept detectors exist — you can find and check them. We turned up dial #1843 by blind search and confirmed it on countries it had never seen (3.1 vs -0.1). Nothing was hand-picked.
  • But you can't just read them off one by one. Always-on dials, half-working detectors, and superposition mean meaning is packed, not filed. That gap is exactly what modern interpretability tools (like sparse autoencoders) are built to close.
Connecting it back

Induction showed attention can run a genuine algorithm. This shows the feed-forward half can hold genuine knowledge — dials that recognize an idea, provably (a “country” dial that fires 3.1 on countries it never saw, ~0 on everything else). Together they're the two moves inside every layer: words share information, then each word is recognized for what it is — repeated a dozen times, that's most of what a model does.
