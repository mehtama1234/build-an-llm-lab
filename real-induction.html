Catching the model copying.

We've said attention lets each word "look at the other words." But that's vague. Can a word ever look at the others in a way that's actually smart — or is it just blurry mixing? Here we catch real GPT-2 doing one clearly clever thing, and then find the exact part of it responsible.

The clever thing we zoomed in on

It can repeat something it just saw

Try this. Write “the secret code is Vpharnix … the secret code is ___”, and the model correctly answers Vpharnix — a made-up word it has never seen while learning. Sit with what that means: the model can't be pulling that word from anything it was taught. The only place it exists is earlier in the text you just gave it. So to answer, it has to notice it earlier and carry it forward.

That's a simple, clear skill, and you can say the rule in one line: find where this happened before, and say whatever came next. Looking back at earlier words is exactly what attention is for — so our question got sharp: is the model really doing this, and can we catch the exact part that does it?

Experiment A — does it really copy?

Give it a string of random, meaningless words, then repeat the exact same string. The second time is impossible to predict — unless the model is copying from the first time.

We use 24 random words (pure noise — nothing to know in advance, nothing it could have memorized) and paste the string twice in a row. Then we simply check, word by word, how often its next-word guess is right:

0%
right the first time through
(random noise — nothing to predict)
96%
right the second time through
(now it can remember the first)

Hopeless the first time, almost perfect the second — and it flips the instant the string repeats. Since the words are random, it can't be using anything it was taught; it's using what it just saw a moment ago. That is the proof: the model copies from earlier in the text.

Experiment B — find the part that does it

The model doesn't do its looking once — it does it many times at once, like a set of separate little “lookers.” So we checked every one and asked: which looker is the one that looks back to “what came next last time”?

There are 144 of these lookers in all — 12 of them at each of 12 stages of depth (12×12=144; their proper name is attention heads). For each, we measured how much of its looking lands on exactly the right earlier spot — the word it would need to copy — and lined them up:

One looker stands out: it spends 94% of its looking on precisely the word to copy. We didn't hand-pick it — going through all 144 turned it up. And these aren't arbitrary: the handful that score highest here — 5·5, 7·10, 5·1, 6·9 — are the same heads interpretability researchers independently singled out as GPT-2's “induction heads.” We found them from scratch, just by measuring.

Now here is a picture of where that one looker looks. Every row is a word doing the looking; a bright square shows the spot it looked at. Watch the bottom half: each word in the repeat reaches straight back to the matching place in the first copy — the same move, every time (outlined):

That neat, regular diagonal is simply what “it follows the same rule every time” looks like when you draw it. There's the copying, laid out in plain sight.

And the same looker on readable made-up words it has never seen — it still copies them out of the prompt:

“…s Vpharnix. I repeat, the secret code is”→ V 0.65
“…to the market. Later that day, Zylthorpe”→ was 0.10

The first pulls the invented code straight from earlier in the text. (The second is a fuzzier case — here the model would rather write a natural continuation than copy literally. This copying move is always competing with everything else the model wants to do, and it doesn't always win.)

Experiment C — is it really doing the work?

Finding a looker that watches the right spot isn't the same as proving it's the one that does the copying. So we switch it off — literally zero out its output — and see if the copying breaks.

First, switch off just that one top looker. Almost nothing happens — the copying holds up. That's the surprise, and it's the honest result: the model doesn't keep one copier, it keeps a whole team of them, so knocking out one leaves the rest to cover. Only when we switch off the whole team of 6 together does the copying fall apart:

nothing removed — the real model
96%
only the single top head (5·5) off
91%
all 6 induction heads off
26%
6 unrelated heads off (control)
87%
2nd-copy accuracy under each knock-out. Removing the 6 copier-heads together sends it from 96% to 26%. Removing 6 unrelated heads barely dents it (87%) — so it's these heads in particular, not just “breaking any 6 heads.”

And on the readable example — the made-up secret code — turning the team off collapses its confidence in the copied word from 64% to 0.6%:

“…the secret code is” → V team on 64% · team off 0.6%

This is the step from correlation to cause. Experiment B showed these heads look at the right place; C shows the copying actually runs through them — remove them and it's gone, remove others and it stays.

Why this is a big deal

It's much of how a model learns from your prompt

“Find where this happened before, and continue it the same way” isn't tied to any particular words — it works on any pattern. That's a large part of why you can teach a model just by typing into the prompt: show it a couple of examples of a format, a list, a style, and it keeps going the same way. It's learning from what you wrote without anyone retraining it — and this look-back-and-continue move is one of the core pieces of how that works.

  • Attention isn't just blurry mixing. A single one of these lookers can carry out a clear, statable rule — and you can find it, measure it, and watch it work.
  • And you can prove it, not just point at it. Switch the copier-heads off and the copying is gone (96%→26%); switch off the same number of other heads and it stays. The behavior lives in these heads — and the model keeps a redundant team of them, not a single point of failure.
  • Nothing here was staged. The 0%→96% jump and the one looker that stood out are direct reads from the real model on random input.
  • This is the small model. It's already clear at this size; in bigger models this copying is stronger and there's more of it.
Connecting it back

Earlier we saw attention as a mechanism — words looking at words. This shows there's real cleverness inside it: one part of a real model does nothing but “copy what came next last time,” proven by going from 0% to 96% on a repeated random string, and drawn as a neat, repeating pattern of where it looks. Attention doesn't just blend words together — pieces of it run genuine little procedures, and this one is much of how a model picks things up from your prompt on the fly.
