Text becomes numbers.

A neural net only does arithmetic. So before anything — before meaning, attention, prediction — the words you type have to become integers. That first, unglamorous step is tokenization, and it quietly decides a shocking amount about how the model behaves. Everything below is the real GPT-2 tokenizer's output, not a diagram.

The idea, in plain words

Chop the text, number the pieces.

The model keeps a fixed dictionary of 50,257 pieces (GPT-2's vocabulary). To feed it a sentence, you split the sentence into those pieces (tokens) and replace each with its dictionary number (its ID). That's the whole job. Here's your sentence, actually run through it — each colored box is one token, the number under it is the exact integer the model receives:

The464·robot9379·picked6497·up510·the262·red2266·block2512.13

Notice the boxes carry a leading · (space). To this tokenizer, ·robot is one unit — the space is part of the token. That tiny fact causes real behavior, as you'll see.

Why this matters

This subword trick (Byte-Pair Encoding) is the whole reason one fixed 50,257-word dictionary can represent any input — English, code, typos, new slang — by assembling known fragments. It's the compromise between two bad extremes, which the next panel shows.

The big-picture tradeoff

Why not just use letters, or whole words?

Three ways to chop the same paragraph. Letters give a tiny dictionary but a very long sequence (and attention cost grows with length²). Whole words keep it short but need a giant, brittle dictionary that chokes on any unseen word. Subword tokens sit in the sweet spot — nearly as short as words, but never stumped:

words19

Same 115 characters of text. BPE lands right near the word count while handling anything you throw at it — that's why every modern model uses it.

Why the model does surprising things

Three quirks that are really just tokenization.

1 · It can't reliably count letters. Ask a model how many r's are in strawberry (there are 3) and it often flubs it — because it never sees letters. It sees:

st301raw1831berry8396

Three chunks, no separate letters. Counting characters inside a token is genuinely hard for it — not stupidity, just what it was handed.

2 · It fumbles long arithmetic. Numbers fracture unpredictably — no consistent per-digit units — so math on big numbers is working against the tokenizer:

A design knob

Bigger dictionary → fewer, cleaner pieces.

Vocabulary size is a real lever. GPT-4's tokenizer knows ~2× more pieces than GPT-2's, so the same rare word breaks into fewer chunks — shorter sequences, but a heavier lookup table. The same word, two tokenizers:

The one-line aha

A model never sees your text — it sees a list of integers pointing into a fixed dictionary of word-fragments. Half of its "quirks" are just the seams where that chopping happened.
