Many heads.

One attention has to squeeze every kind of relationship — grammar, meaning, position — into a single set of weights. That's too much for one lens. So a transformer runs several attentions in parallel (heads), each free to look for a different relationship, then merges what they found. Real per-head weights below.

The problem with one

A single head can only make one choice

When cat gathers context, does it want the nearby word, the matching noun, the describing adjective, or the subject of the sentence? A single attention has to pick one blended weighting. But those are different jobs — and a token often needs several at once.

In a trained model, heads specialize

Here the projections are random (to show heads can differ). In a real trained GPT, they're learned, and heads take on interpretable jobs: one head reliably attends to the previous token, another links verbs to their subjects, another tracks matching brackets or rare words. Different heads, different relationships — discovered, not assigned.

The mechanism

Split → attend in parallel → concatenate → mix

You don't pay full price for each head. The token's vector is split into h smaller slices (768 dims → 12 heads × 64), each head does its own Q/K/V attention on its slice, then the outputs are concatenated back to full size and passed through one final projection that mixes them:

token vector · 768
↓ split into h slices
head 1
Q·K·V → attnhead 2
Q·K·V → attnhead 3
…… head h
↓ concatenate the outputs
→ final projection (mixes heads)
↓
new token vector · 768

Total compute is about the same as one big head — you just divide the dimensions among the heads instead of adding more.

The one-line aha

One head forces one answer to "what's relevant?"; many heads let a token ask that question several ways at once — then combine the answers. That parallel-of-relationships is why attention is so expressive.
