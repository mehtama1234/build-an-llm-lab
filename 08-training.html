Where the numbers come from.

Every model in this lab so far arrived pre-trained — its weights already meaningful. But those weights start as pure random noise. Training is the process that turns noise into a model. Here we train a tiny transformer from scratch on a clean task and watch it learn — and stumble into one of the strangest, most revealing things a network does: it memorizes first, and only much later actually understands.

The task and the tools

Learn to add, mod 97

We give a small transformer (422,241 weights, all random to start) every fact of the form a + b (mod 97) — but show it only 3,763 of the 9,409 facts and hold out the other 5,646. Can it learn the rule from a fraction of the examples, or will it just memorize the ones it saw?

Training itself is two lines, repeated:

loss = −log p(correct answer) # how surprised the model was by the truth
weight ← weight − η · ∂loss/∂weight # nudge every weight to reduce that surprise

Measure the loss, compute how each weight affects it, step every weight downhill, repeat. Do it enough times and random noise becomes a working model. But watch what "working" means over time.

For the whole shaded stretch the model looked "done" by the usual measure — perfect on its training data — while being useless on anything new. Understanding arrived 26× later, in a sudden jump. This delayed leap is called grokking.
Why it happens

Memorizing is easy; understanding is simpler

There are two ways to be perfect on the training facts: build a giant lookup table (memorize each answer), or discover the actual rule of modular addition. The lookup table is easy to find, so the model finds it first. But we also apply a gentle pressure called weight decay — a small tug on every weight toward zero, so the model prefers solutions that use smaller, simpler machinery. The lookup table is complex and heavy; the true rule is compact and light. So over many steps that steady pressure squeezes the model out of the bulky memorized solution and into the elegant general one — and the instant it does, it works on facts it never saw. Grokking is that phase change, caught on camera.

Why this matters
  • Training is just loss and gradients. Measure surprise, step every weight downhill, repeat millions of times — that single loop is how every weight in every model on this site was made.
  • Low training loss is not understanding. A model can be flawless on what it was shown and clueless on everything else. Memorization and generalization are different things, and they can happen at very different times.
  • Generalization can be a sudden phase change. The model doesn't ease into understanding; it snaps into it once the pressure toward simplicity wins. Learning has structure and drama, not just a smooth curve.
The lab, from noise to knowing

You've now seen the full life of a model: random numbers, trained by nothing but loss-and-gradient into something that first memorizes, then — under a quiet pull toward simplicity — groks the rule and generalizes to facts it never met. Train accuracy hit 100% by step ~100; real understanding arrived at step ~2,600. That gap is the difference between remembering and knowing, made visible.
