Build-an-LLM · Lab · The mental model

One running vector.

Forget the tangle of boxes and arrows for a minute. Underneath, a language model is doing something you can actually hold in your head: it keeps one list of numbers for each word, and every part of the model does nothing but add small changes to that list until it's good enough to guess the next word. This page builds that picture slowly, in plain words — with the details that usually get skipped.

Start here

The model has exactly one job

Look at the words so far, and guess the next word. That's it.

Not translate, not reason, not hold a conversation — those all fall out of getting extremely good at this one guess, on a scale of billions of examples. Every piece we describe below earns its place in only one way: it makes that next guess a little better. So whenever a part seems complicated, ask the one question that cuts through it — how does this help guess the next word? — and it stops being arbitrary.

The thing that gets refined

Each word becomes a list of numbers

Before anything else happens, every word is turned into a list of numbers — a few hundred of them. That list is the word's current description. At the very start it only says two things: what the word is, and where it sits in the sentence.

Here is the whole trick of the model, stated once: it spends all its effort improving that list. By the time the model is done, the same list for the word cat will no longer just say "the word cat" — it will describe what cat means in this exact sentence: whose cat, doing what, mentioned how. The model is the process that carries the list from the first meaning to the second.

The real name: that list of numbers is the word's vector. How many numbers are in it is the model's width (768 for the smallest GPT-2).
The idea most explanations skip

Every step adds — it never replaces

This is the key, so slow down here. Each step of the model does not throw away the word's list and build a fresh one. It takes the current list, works out a small set of changes, and adds those changes on top of what's already there.

Follow that all the way through and you get the single most useful picture of a transformer: there is one list per word that runs unbroken from the start of the model to the end, and every part of the model simply reads it and adds to it. Like this, for one word:

the very first description can ride straight down to the bottom, untouched, unless a step deliberately cancels it
list for “cat” — what it is, where it sits
move · attention  + what the other words offer
now bent by the sentence around it
transform · feed-forward  + what it works out on its own
refined a little
move · attention  + gather again, with better eyes
transform · feed-forward  + compute again
…this pair repeats, layer after layer…
final list — a full description in context
↓ read off → guess the next word

Three things follow from "add, don't replace," and together they are much of why the model works at all:

  • Nothing is lost by force. The starting description can travel the whole way down untouched. Later steps only lose it if they actively add something that cancels it out — losing information becomes a choice, not an accident of passing through.
  • Each step has an easy job. A step never has to rebuild the word from nothing. It only has to decide what small thing to add. Easy jobs are easy to learn.
  • The steps aren't fragile links in a chain. They all pour into the same shared list, so order is forgiving — you can often remove a step entirely and the rest still works, because nothing downstream depended on that one step being there.
The real name: that shared, added-to list is the residual stream — "residual" because each step contributes a residual, a leftover change, instead of a replacement.
What the steps actually are

Only two kinds of step, taking turns

Everything happening to that running list is one of just two moves, alternating the whole way down:

move · attention
thecatsaton
A word looks at the other words and pulls in a blend of whatever it finds useful. This is the only step where words see each other. It moves information sideways, between words. It barely does any math to it — it mostly just gathers and mixes.
transform · feed-forward
catwork on itcat′
Each word is worked on entirely on its own, with no looking at other words at all. This is where the real computing happens — where a word's gathered-up description gets turned into something more useful.

They take turns: gather from the other words, then process on your own, then gather again, then process again. Said plainly: attention is the model's only way for words to talk to each other; feed-forward is the model's only way to actually compute. That's why you need both — one moves information, the other thinks about it. Neither can do the other's job.

Opening up the "compute" step

Feed-forward: widen, bend, narrow

Let's take apart the transform step, because it's where most of the model actually lives. It's three simple parts in a row:

word list
~few hundred
→ widen ×4 →
a much wider list
+ a bend on each number
→ narrow →
back to original size
(ready to add on)

Widen: blow the list up to about four times as many numbers — room to check for many different patterns at once. Bend: push each number through a simple curve (roughly: let positive values through, squash negatives toward zero). Narrow: squeeze back down to the original size, so the result can be added onto the running list.

Detail 1 — the bend is the whole point, not a flourish

Widening and narrowing are both "straight-line" operations. And two straight-line steps in a row always collapse into a single straight-line step — you truly gain nothing by having two. The little bend in the middle is what breaks that collapse. It is the only reason stacking many layers buys you anything. Remove the bend, and a 96-layer model would be exactly as capable as a 1-layer model — no more.

Detail 2 — most of the model is here, not in attention

Attention gets the spotlight, but if you count the actual numbers the model learns, roughly two-thirds of them sit in these widen/narrow steps, not in attention. The bulk of what a model "knows" is stored in feed-forward.

Detail 3 — what the wide middle is doing

Think of each of those extra slots in the widened list as a tiny detector. It watches the word's description for one particular pattern; when it finds it, it switches on and adds its own specific contribution to the running list. There are far more patterns worth watching for than there are slots — so the detectors overlap and share duty, which is exactly why you usually can't point at one slot and say cleanly what it means.

Why "add, don't replace" really matters

The reason is about how it learns

We keep saying every step adds rather than replaces. Here's the deep reason — and it's about training, not the forward guess.

The model learns like this: after each guess, it gets told how every one of its numbers should change to make the guess a bit better. That correction has to travel backwards through every step — from the final guess all the way back to the very first step.

if each step replaced the list
correction
× step × step × step …
→ shrinks toward 0

Multiplying by many small amounts in a row crushes it. By the time it reaches the early steps, there's nothing left — so the early steps never learn. In a deep model, fatal.

if each step adds instead
a straight path skips each step's math
→ correction arrives intact

Because adding leaves an untouched path straight through, the correction reaches every step whole. Now even the earliest steps can learn.

That one choice — add, don't replace — is what makes a deep model trainable at all. We watched it happen for real in Session 6: push a list through 40 steps. Replace each time → the signal dies to zero, nothing learns. Add each time → it survives — but with nothing holding it back it balloons to millions. Which forces the last piece.

Keeping it steady

Resizing before each step

Adding at every step keeps the signal alive, but it also lets the numbers grow bigger and bigger as they pile up — and numbers that are too big make the next steps misbehave. So before each step reads the list, the model resizes it: shift and scale the numbers so they sit at a standard, predictable size.

The nuance people miss

Right after resizing, the model is allowed to stretch the numbers back out however it likes. So resizing is not forcing the numbers into some fixed shape — it only keeps their overall size under control, while leaving the model free to shape them as it wants. The goal is steadiness, not conformity.

  • It looks only at that one word's own numbers — never at the other words. That independence is why it works no matter how long the sentence is.
  • Where you put it matters. Modern models resize only the copy that feeds into each step, and never touch the running list itself — so the clean, add-only path stays clean from top to bottom.
The real name: this resizing is normalization (you'll see "LayerNorm" / "RMSNorm").
Doing it many times

Stacking — refinement, not repetition

Now take the whole bundle — move, transform, add, resize — and stack it many times. GPT-2 stacks it 12 to 48 times; larger models, more. Three things to get right, because each is a common misread:

L1 L2 L3 L4 L5 … L12
  • The stacked copies are not the same copy reused. Each layer has its own separate numbers. "Twelve layers" means twelve independent sets of numbers, each free to take on a different job. (Older models reused one piece over and over — transformers do not.)
  • Depth is refinement, not repetition. Because the "move" step runs fresh at every layer, a later layer can look across the words using distinctions an earlier layer already worked out. So it goes: gather with basic eyes → understand a little → gather again with sharper eyes → understand more. Each layer re-reads the sentence, better informed than the last.
  • All words go through a layer together. Every word is processed at the same time, not one after another. The depth is in the stack of layers — not in marching along the sentence.
Getting the answer out

Reading the last list as a guess

After the final layer, each word's list has become a rich description of that word in this exact sentence. To guess what comes next, the model takes the list belonging to the last word, and scores every possible next word against it. Turn those scores into percentages, and that's the guess. Pick one, stick it on the end, and run the entire thing again for the word after that.

The whole thing, once, in plain words

Each word is a running list of numbers. Attention lets words hand information to each other; feed-forward lets each word be computed on. Every step adds its result instead of replacing — so nothing is lost, and the model can actually learn. Resizing keeps the numbers steady. This repeats across many independent layers, each refining the lists a little more. At the end, the last word's list is read off as a guess at the next word.

And the part worth sitting with: none of the grammar, meaning, or apparent reasoning is built in. It's simply what the numbers settle into after being nudged, billions of times, toward one goal — guess the next word.
